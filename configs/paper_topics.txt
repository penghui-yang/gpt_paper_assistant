1. New methodological improvements or theoretical analysis about inference acceleration of large language models, especially about speculative decoding. Speculative decoding is a specific method for accelerating the inference of auto-regressive models, which drafts several future tokens efficiently and then verifies them in parallel.
    - Relevant: Papers that discuss specific methods for inference acceleration of LLMs, propose improvements to existing acceleration methods, or analyze these methods theoretically. Papers that explicitly mention speculative decoding are highly appreciated.
    - Not relevant: Papers mainly about adaptation to a specific task. Simply following instructions or inputs is not sufficient.

2. New methodological improvements or theoretical analysis in decoding techniques for large language models (LLMs). Decoding is the process used by language models to generate text based on the input sequence and the model's learned parameters.
   - Relevant: Papers that discuss novel decoding methods, improvements to existing decoding techniques, or theoretical analyses of decoding processes in LLMs. This includes research on optimizing decoding strategies for better model performance, new algorithms for decoding, or studies demonstrating the impact of different decoding methods on language model outputs. Approaches that combine decoding with other model components to enhance overall efficiency and accuracy are also relevant. Papers that explicitly mention speculative decoding are highly appreciated.
   - Not relevant: Papers that primarily focus on applications of language models without discussing the decoding process in detail, or papers that only mention decoding in passing without contributing new methods or significant improvements.

3. New methodological improvements or theoretical analysis about scaling laws for large language models. Scaling laws describe how model performance, loss, sample efficiency, compute efficiency, or emergent capabilities change as a function of model size, dataset size, compute budget, training duration, architecture, or other scaling variables.
   - Relevant: Papers that propose new scaling laws, refine existing scaling law formulations, provide theoretical explanations for observed scaling behavior, or empirically study how performance changes under different scaling regimes. This includes work on compute-optimal training, data-constrained scaling, inference-time scaling, scaling behavior across domains or tasks, and surprising empirical deviations from standard scaling predictions.
   - Not relevant: Papers that merely train a larger model without analyzing scaling behavior. Papers that report benchmark improvements from scale alone are not relevant unless they include methodological or theoretical insights about scaling.

4. New methodological improvements or theoretical analysis about data selection, data ordering, data mixture, or curriculum design for training large language models. This category focuses on how the choice, composition, quality, diversity, sequencing, and weighting of training data affect model performance, generalization, efficiency, and scaling behavior.
   - Relevant: Papers that propose new methods for selecting or filtering training data, designing data mixtures, reweighting datasets, ordering examples during training, constructing curricula, or analyzing the interaction between data composition and model behavior. This includes work on optimal data mixtures, domain balancing, synthetic-versus-human data trade-offs, deduplication, quality filtering, data attribution, active data selection, and empirical or theoretical studies of how data choices affect scaling laws or downstream generalization.
   - Not relevant: Papers that introduce a dataset without analyzing its role in training dynamics or model performance. Papers that only use a standard dataset mixture as an implementation detail are not relevant unless they provide a meaningful methodological or theoretical contribution about data selection, ordering, or mixture design.

In suggesting papers to your friend, remember that he enjoys papers on statistical machine learning and generative modeling in natural language processing.
Your friend also likes learning about surprising empirical results in language models, as well as clever statistical tricks.
